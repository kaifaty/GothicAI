# Learned (neural, non-text) behaviour policies for large NPC populations: state of the art as of 2026-11-04

Method note for the report writer. Numbers come from primary papers where I could read them (arXiv HTML/ar5iv versions, and the GT Sophy Nature paper through a local text extraction of the author-hosted PDF). Where I only saw a search-result snippet or a secondary article I say so in the bullet. "Inference" bullets are my own reasoning or arithmetic, not sourced claims. Direct PDF fetching through the web tool failed (binary), so a few papers (Neural MMO 1.0 full text beyond ar5iv, Melting Pot 1, Nature supplementary) were read only partly.

## 1. Multi-agent RL at scale with survival/needs: what emerged, how much compute, what broke

### Takeaway
Population-scale MARL with survival pressure works at 100 to 1,000 agents with small shared-weight networks (50k to 1.6M parameters), and produces niches, exploration, specialisation and tool use, but only after 10^8 to 10^9 agent-episodes or steps, and every large result needed hand-built curricula, reward shaping or action masks. No source I found trains a "society" (relationships, property, tasks passing between agents) from scratch with RL; the societal results (roles, taxes, religion) come from LLM-agent simulations, which cost orders of magnitude more per decision.

### Cited Findings
**Neural MMO**
- Neural MMO 1.0 (2019): populations with unshared weights tested at 1, 2, 4, 8; spawn cap 16 x Npop, so up to 16/32/64/128 concurrent agents; parameters shared across groups of up to 16 agents; 80x80 procedural maps, 100 worlds per experiment — [Suarez et al., Neural MMO (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1903.00784)
- Neural MMO 1.0 policies were small fully connected nets with 50-100k parameters; observation was a 15x15 tile crop plus per-agent attributes (lifetime, health, food, water, position, last damage...); reward was +1 per tick alive with discount 0.99 and nothing else; trained with REINFORCE + value baseline, Adam lr 1e-3, entropy 1e-2 — [same](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1903.00784)
- Compute for 1.0: the environment was tested with "up to 100 million agent trajectories (lifetimes) on 100 cores in 1 week" — [same](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1903.00784)
- What emerged in 1.0: exploration (map coverage rises with more concurrent agents, because of competitive pressure), niche formation (species spread into separate regions as the number of populations grows), policies that depend on other agents' positions, keeping distance and striking first when melee was made overpowered — [same](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1903.00784)
- What failed or was weak in 1.0: small populations learned brittle policies that did not generalise to more competitive settings; training was less stable without discounting; larger batches were always more stable; learned targeting and attention architectures were only "preliminary" and noisy — [same](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1903.00784)
- Neural MMO 2.0 (NeurIPS 2023 D&B): 128 agents per map in the standard setting (users control 8); engine simulates about 3,000 agent steps per CPU core per second (vs 800-1,000 in 1.x), random-action benchmark, dead agents excluded; one model trainable in about 8 A100 GPU-hours (compute from Stability AI, CarperAI, EleutherAI); PPO via CleanRL with PufferLib; task system with float-valued predicates (partial credit) and 25 built-in predicates — [Neural MMO 2.0 (arXiv HTML)](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.03736)
- Neural MMO 2.0 stated limitations: no new mechanics vs 1.x, limited agent specialisation within teams, "an overly broad survival objective that favors dominant strategies", team tasks postponed; in the prior competition top approaches "did not learn to comprehend and utilize all of the game systems" — [same](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.03736)
- I found no Neural MMO 3.0 numbers; PufferLib's README gives no throughput figures (only "train tiny, super-human models in seconds") — [PufferLib repo](https://github.com/PufferAI/PufferLib)

**OpenAI hide-and-seek (emergent tool use)**
- Default model about 1.6M parameters (entity embeddings 128, residual self-attention 4 heads x 32, mean-pool over entities, MLP 256, LSTM 256); PPO, parameters shared across agents, centralised omniscient value function — [Baker et al. (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1909.07528)
- Compute: 132.3M episodes (31.7B frames) over about 34 hours to reach stage 4 (ramp defence), batch 64,000; stage 5 (box surfing) took about 380M episodes in total; GPU/CPU counts not stated — [same](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1909.07528)
- Emergence order: running/chasing, fort building (about 25M episodes), ramp use (about 75M more), ramp defence (about 10M more); the paper's stage counts (25+75+10 = about 110M) do not reconcile with the 132.3M headline figure — [same](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1909.07528)
- Negative results: batch sizes 8,000 and 16,000 never converged; masked value function never got past stage 3; pooling-only architectures less sample-efficient than self-attention; a food variant failed to produce food-focused behaviour; agents exploited the physics engine (box surfing, leaving the play area) — [same](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1909.07528)

**Salesforce AI Economist**
- Gather-Trade-Build: 4 or 10 agents (25x25 and 40x40 grids), 1,000 steps per episode in 10 tax periods; one-step economy with N = 100 agents; agent net: 2 conv layers, FC 128, LSTM 128 (planner FC/LSTM 256); weights shared across agents; 30 environment replicas on 15 CPUs; phase 1 25M or 50M steps (table and text disagree), phase 2 1B steps — [Zheng et al. (arXiv HTML)](https://arxiv.org/html/2108.02755v1)
- Utility is isoelastic in coins minus labour cost (eta = 0.23; labour: move 0.21, gather 0.21, trade 0.05, build 2.1); specialisation emerged (high build-skill agents become builders, low-skill gatherers); AI tax policy improved equality x productivity by 12% (4 agents) and 8.6% (10 agents) over the Saez baseline; agents learned tax-gaming (shifting income across periods) — [same](https://arxiv.org/html/2108.02755v1)
- The authors call two-level RL "highly unstable and novel"; stabilisers were a curriculum (tax-free first, tax annealed over 27M steps), planner entropy annealed over 50M steps, and action masks — [same](https://arxiv.org/html/2108.02755v1)

**DeepMind Melting Pot**
- Melting Pot 2.0 has over 50 substrates and over 250 test scenarios; player counts per substrate 4 to 16 (e.g. Allelopathic Harvest 16, Clean Up 7); each agent trained 10^9 steps, 3 seeds, TPU learner (6 chips); baselines ACB / V-MPO / OPRE with a small net (conv 16/32, MLP 64x2, LSTM 128) — [Melting Pot 2.0 (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2211.13746)
- Findings: ACB best on average (credit to PopArt); collective-reward (prosocial) variants underperformed self-interested ones overall, though they won on some dilemmas (Clean Up, Commons Harvest); scores near random on Collaborative Cooking, Running with Scissors, Paintball CTF; no algorithm learned to cooperate in Coins — [same](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2211.13746)

**Needs/homeostasis in learned agents**
- ForageWorld (Craftax-based, 96x96 grid, 9x11 view): hunger, thirst, fatigue, health; sleep only possible below 50% energy; cows deplete; predators. PPO with a GRU core (512 hidden, magnitude-pruned to 90% sparsity). Reward = 0.1 x (1 + sign(health-5) + sign(food-5) + sign(drink-5) + sign(energy-5)), i.e. paid for keeping each need above half, not for maximising it — [ForageWorld (arXiv HTML)](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.06981)
- ForageWorld results: spiral exploration, then direct return to remembered food patches, patch choice weighed by recency/predator encounters/cow counts; allocentric position decodable 50-100 steps into the past/future; expert rates: eat about once per 95 steps, drink 0.034 per step, sleep 0.262 per step; feed-forward nets and a 64-unit RNN performed significantly worse; removing the auxiliary position loss hurt in large arenas. Training compute not reported in the text I could read — [same](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.06981)
- Chalmers master's thesis on a predator-prey grid with survival constraints: homeostatic reward "might improve early learning" but results were not statistically significant (weak evidence, a thesis) — [Chalmers ODR](https://odr.chalmers.se/items/49acc9d2-22e9-4650-b43b-b07c9097c28c)
- Sugarscape and RL: Jäger (2021) proposes agents that learn their rules through a process close to RL with a utility function instead of hand-written rules, and replicates Sugarscape as proof of principle; Brearcliffe and Crooks compare evolutionary computation, Q-learning and SARSA in Sugarscape and conclude that learning does not always give better results and that attributes the modeller thinks important may not matter to the agent (both from search snippets, not read in full) — [Jäger, IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/nmcmxx/v27y2021i1p162-178.html); [Brearcliffe and Crooks preprint](https://easychair.org/publications/preprint/w3H1)

**Large-population simulation with LLM agents (the contrast case)**
- Project Sid (Altera, Oct 2024): PIANO architecture with about 10 concurrent modules, "reflex modules use small, fast non-LLM neural networks", GPT-4o for deliberation; 25 to 50 agents for most experiments, 30 per village for specialisation, one 500-agent run over 9,000 s; over 1,000 agents exceeded the Minecraft server limits; social goals regenerated every 5-10 s; roles (farmer, miner, guard, explorer) emerged and persisted, but without social modules roles were homogeneous and unstable; constituents paid about 20% of inventory in tax, about 9% after the rate was cut to 5-10% — [Project Sid (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2411.00114)
- Project Sid failure modes: hallucinations compound across successive LLM calls and can make an agent stop pursuing needs such as food; agents get stuck in repetitive loops; miscommunication between output streams spreads errors; "lack robust innate drives, such as survival or curiosity"; per-agent cost and latency not reported — [same](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2411.00114)
- Generative Agents (Smallville, 25 agents): a secondary summary says the two-day run cost "thousands of dollars" in token credit; another unverified site estimates about 6,000 LLM calls and $50-300 at current prices; the numbers conflict and neither is from the paper — [summary of talk](https://pumacp.github.io/puma-vault/20---Literature/20.1-Papers/Agent-Architectures/LN-Park-2023-GenerativeAgents); cost-reduction follow-up [Affordable Generative Agents](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.02053v2)
- AgentTorch / Large Population Models (MIT): 8.4M synthetic NYC residents; tensorised, differentiable agent-based simulation; for LLM behaviour it queries K archetypes x A actions x M samples and lets each agent sample from its archetype's action distribution instead of one LLM call per agent; prior LLM-agent work is described as limited to 25-1,000 agents; claimed speedups 600x (simulation), 3,000x (calibration), 5,000x (analysis) but the paper's own Figure 10 times (100,000 h to 20 min; 5,000 h to 10 s) imply far larger ratios, so the multipliers are inconsistent and are the authors' own claims — [Large Population Models (arXiv HTML)](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.09901v1)
- AgentTorch limitations: archetype sampling is independent per agent so it misses within-household correlation; gradient estimates for discrete choices have high variance; numerical instability when combining substeps — [same](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.09901v1)
- FLAME GPU 2 (agent-based modelling on GPU): Sugarscape-style socio-economic model with up to 16M agents reported; 3.5x and 10x speedups for ensembles and concurrent execution over a baseline (search-snippet level) — [FLAME GPU 2 paper record](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/199416/)
- MAgent: "can host up to one million agents on a single GPU server" (abstract claim, mentions communication, leadership and altruism as observable phenomena) — [MAgent](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.00600)

### Inferences
- The closest empirical precedent for "one small shared net drives hundreds of needy agents" is Neural MMO plus ForageWorld: 50k to 500k parameters, survival-only or threshold-based homeostatic reward, recurrence needed for memory of resource locations. That is the same size class as the user's existing combat and navigation nets.
- Everything "social" (relationships, property, delegation of tasks, taxes, religion) has been shown only with LLM agents or with a hand-designed economic game (AI Economist, 4 to 10 agents). A learned society of 1,000 would be new research; the practical route is a learned chooser inside a designed world where the code owns the graph (relationships, ownership) and exposes it as observations.
- Sample budgets of 10^8 to 10^9 steps (Neural MMO 1.0, hide-and-seek, Melting Pot) are the right order of magnitude to plan for when training from scratch; this is why simulator throughput (Neural MMO 2.0: about 3,000 agent-steps per core-second) matters more than network size. The user's lockstep engine simulation would have to be much faster, or a lightweight surrogate world used for most training.
- Emergence claims in Project Sid come from LLM priors, not from de novo learning, and the authors say so ("cannot yet show de novo emergence of institutions").

### Gaps
- Neural MMO 3.0 throughput and training-time numbers: not found in readable sources.
- Compute for ForageWorld (GPU, hours): not in the part of the paper I could read.
- No RL-trained population of 100+ agents with explicit relationship graphs, ownership and task transfer found (2023-2026 searches returned only LLM-agent societies and small toy economies).
- Melting Pot 1 headline claims (21 substrates / 85 scenarios) appear only in a secondary article; I used 2.0 numbers from the primary paper.

## 2. Architectures for many interacting agents

### Takeaway
The dominant pattern is one shared network for all agents (parameter sharing), conditioned on a per-agent input (own state, id or embedding), with entity-based observation encoders: either masked mean/max pooling over nearby entities or residual self-attention over an entity set, and a pointer head that picks one entity out of a variable-size list for "choose an object" actions. Graph/communication nets (DGN, MAGIC) and mean-field approximations exist to scale to larger agent counts; sharing is the trade-off against behavioural heterogeneity.

### Cited Findings
- Hide-and-seek: entity embeddings of 128 units (same-type entities share weights, concatenated with the agent's own state), residual self-attention 4 heads x 32 without position embeddings, masked mean-pool, MLP 256, LSTM 256, about 1.6M parameters; pooling-only variants were less sample-efficient but slightly faster per wall-clock second — [Baker et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1909.07528)
- Neural MMO 1.0: tile indices embedded to 7-d, entity attributes projected to 32-d and max-pooled over entities to handle a variable number of agents; one linear layer main network, separate softmax heads for movement and attack — [Suarez et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1903.00784)
- AlphaStar entity encoder (as described in the Perceiver IO paper): Transformer with 3 attention layers, 2 heads, feature dimension 128, a 256-channel 1D conv for per-entity embeddings; the target-unit head computes keys from entity embeddings and uses a pointer network to pick one entity, invariant to the number of entities; the target is one of seven autoregressively sampled arguments — [Perceiver IO](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.14795); [AlphaStar walkthrough blog](https://xlnwel.github.io/blog/reinforcement%20learning/AlphaStar). A secondary wiki gives about 139M total parameters and 55M at inference; unverified — [aiwiki](https://www.aiwiki.ai/wiki/alphastar)
- OpenAI Five: about 159M parameters, 84% in a single 4,096-unit LSTM; acts every 4th frame at 30 fps (about 7.5 steps per second); each hero a replica of the same network — [OpenAI Five (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1912.06680)
- Mean-field MARL: factorises the Q-function into pairwise agent-neighbour terms and replaces neighbours by their mean action, so the Q-function size does not depend on N; experiments: Gaussian Squeeze with N = 100, 500, 1000 (at 500 and 1000 all four baselines failed to learn, mean-field methods learned the optimal allocation), battle game 64 v 64 and also 8, 144, 256 per army; one shared three-layer MLP Q-network across agents that takes an agent embedding as input — [Yang et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1802.05438)
- DGN (graph convolutional RL): each agent is a node, its encoded observation the node feature, attention as the convolution kernel, one shared parameter set for all agents so it scales to more agents without per-agent networks — [DGN](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1810.09202v4); MAGIC adds a learned scheduler (when/whom to talk to) and a graph-attention message processor on dynamic graphs, 27.4% more communication-efficient than baselines (authors' claim, abstract level) — [MAGIC repo](https://www.github.com/CORE-Robotics-Lab/MAGIC); [2026 survey of GNN-communication MARL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.25972)
- HetGPPO: removing parameter sharing from a GNN-based PPO (GPPO) lets agent policies diverge; sharing is what gives GNN permutation equivariance; the authors argue homogeneous models can imitate heterogeneous behaviour only brittlely and that HetGPPO is more robust to noise — [Bettini et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.07137)
- Preference/weight-conditioned single networks: envelope Q-learning (Yang et al.) optimises Q over actions and weights; Castelletti et al. feed the weight vector as an extra input to a generalised Q-function; PD-MORL (ICLR 2023) uses one policy network aligned to preference vectors; 2026 work on preference-conditioned PPO (D3PO) reports mode collapse and incomplete coverage of the Pareto front in such single policies and adds a diversity regulariser — [D3PO and related-work summary](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.07764v1); [PD-MORL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2208.07914v3.pdf) (snippet-level for PD-MORL)
- Recurrence matters when state is hidden: in ForageWorld feed-forward nets and a 64-unit RNN did markedly worse than a 512-unit GRU — [ForageWorld](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.06981)
- AgentTorch handles population-level heterogeneity with K archetypes per population and lets assignments change over time; each agent is a state vector over static and dynamic properties — [Large Population Models](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.09901v1)

### Inferences
- For the user's "each NPC is a node with needs, goals, relationships" design, the architecture most directly supported by evidence is: shared weights, input = own need/trait vector + an entity set (nearby objects, known NPCs, owned items, open tasks, each as a feature row with a type tag) encoded by masked pooling or 1-2 attention layers, output = a score per candidate (pointer head over the set). AlphaStar's target head and hide-and-seek's masked entity encoder are the working precedents; Neural MMO showed the scalability of the pooling variant.
- Because candidate objects/activities are masked entities, the action space is variable-size by construction and the pointer head needs no per-NPC output layer. Relationships and ownership become edge features or extra rows in the entity set; a full GNN message-passing step is only justified if NPCs must react to each other's intentions, and DGN/MAGIC evidence is mostly on small, cooperative benchmarks.
- Per-NPC personality should be an input vector, not separate weights (HetGPPO shows separate weights give diversity but break permutation equivariance and multiply parameters by N). The multi-objective literature suggests conditioning on a need-weight vector, with the caveat that conditioned policies tend to mode-collapse and need a diversity or coverage check.
- Mean-field input (average of neighbours' actions or states) is a cheap route to crowd-level awareness (queues at the well, who is eating) without attention over everyone.

### Gaps
- No source compared pooling vs attention vs GNN for a needs/daily-life task specifically.
- AlphaStar parameter counts and head sizes: only secondary/derived descriptions (Nature paper not readable here).
- Evidence that a weight-conditioned policy yields stable believable personalities in a game setting: none found.

## 3. Training approaches: RL, imitation/distillation, offline RL, DAgger, hierarchy, learned utility weights

### Takeaway
Pure RL from a designed reward is proven at the 10^8 to 10^9 step scale but needs curricula and reward engineering; imitation from a rule system or LLM teacher is cheaper and more controllable but suffers compounding errors, which DAgger-style iteration addresses. A learned high-level chooser over scripted low-level skills is well motivated but I found no published evaluation specific to NPC daily life.

### Cited Findings
- GT Sophy (Sony AI, Nature 2022): QR-SAC, 4 hidden layers x 2,048 ReLU units for both Q-functions and the policy (October version), dropout 0.1 on the policy, Adam lr 5e-5 (Q) and 2.5e-5 (policy), gamma 0.9896; "mixed-scenario training" injected noisy variants of critical situations (slipstream passing, etc.) alongside full races; ablations show scenario training, multiple replay buffers and a passing reward are all important — [Wurman et al., Nature 2022 (author-hosted PDF)](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~pstone/Papers/bib2html-links/nature22.pdf)
- GT Sophy training infrastructure: typically trained from scratch with 10-20 PlayStations (each instance controls up to 20 cars), an equal number of compute nodes and one GPU trainer (V100 or half an A100, about 8 vCPUs); time-trial runs used 10 rollout workers for about 8 days; racing-in-traffic runs used 21 workers for 7-12 days; 15 seeds all reached superhuman lap times, most in about 10 days, the slowest in 25 days; more than 45,000 driving hours accumulated — [same](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~pstone/Papers/bib2html-links/nature22.pdf)
- DAgger: iterative training of a stationary deterministic policy as a no-regret online learner, with a guarantee of good performance "under the distribution of observations it induces", motivated by the failure of i.i.d. supervised learning in sequential prediction — [Ross, Gordon, Bagnell](https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.0686)
- Distilling LLM agents: trajectories from a teacher used to train a small student (behaviour cloning); common failure is error compounding over long horizons; KAIST/KRAFTON work reports 0.5B/1.5B/3B small LMs matching next-tier larger models after distillation with retrieval and code tools — [Distilling LLM Agent into Small Models](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.17612v1); [Structured Agent Distillation](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.13820v3). These are LM-to-LM distillations, not LLM-to-small-MLP policies.
- ORACLE (CVM 2026): a Transformer-CVAE with contrastive learning that generates 24-hour NPC indoor activity plans, trained on the CASAS smart-home dataset (imbalanced, few samples); the authors argue LLMs lack routine-specific data and produce generic schedules; no model size or inference cost in the abstract — [ORACLE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23933)
- LLM-to-policy compression in practice: Project Sid mixes slow LLM deliberation with small non-LLM reflex nets; AgentTorch replaces per-agent LLM calls with sampling from per-archetype action distributions (K x A x M queries) — [Project Sid](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2411.00114); [Large Population Models](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.09901v1)
- Hierarchy: a team-sports game simulation study has a high-level policy choose latent skill variables for teammates with independent-RL low-level policies; Gehring et al. use pre-trained skills with a three-level policy, framing the skill prior as a sample-efficiency vs generality trade-off (robotics); Andersen et al. report large sample-complexity gains from learning over options on Montezuma's Revenge — [Yang, Borovikov, Zha](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10162642); [Hierarchical Skills](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.10809); [Andersen et al.](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/1802.06604v1) (all snippet-level)
- Learned utility weights: in the AI Economist the agent utility is a fixed designed function (isoelastic income minus labour cost) and the policy is learned; envelope Q-learning and weight-conditioned Q/policy nets learn one policy for a family of weightings — [AI Economist](https://arxiv.org/html/2108.02755v1); [MORL summary](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.07764v1)
- Offline RL: "AlphaStar Unplugged" exists as a large-scale offline-RL benchmark on StarCraft II; I did not read its numbers — [AlphaStar Unplugged](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.03526)
- Training-time engineering: JaxMARL reports up to 12,500x wall-clock speedup versus conventional pipelines by running environments and training on the GPU in JAX (authors' claim; depends on baseline and on vectorised runs) — [JaxMARL](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.10090v5)
- Neural MMO's practical lesson on batch size: larger batches were always more stable; hide-and-seek runs at batch 8k-16k never converged while 64k did — [Suarez et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1903.00784); [Baker et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1909.07528)

### Inferences
- A staged recipe is consistent with the evidence: (1) behaviour-clone a small shared net from the current teacher (rule/utility system or the 2B Decider logging its choices) on states the net itself visits, using DAgger-style relabelling; (2) fine-tune with RL on a homeostatic reward plus an anchor to the teacher so degenerate equilibria do not appear; (3) keep a rule-based override for rare, high-stakes decisions. GT Sophy's mixed-scenario training is the analogue of oversampling rare situations (starvation edge cases, theft, a task handed off).
- For LLM-teacher imitation, the AgentTorch archetype trick (query the teacher for a few archetype x situation cells, then train/sample from the distribution) is a cheap way to cut teacher calls by orders of magnitude; the teacher's cost is paid once at training time, not per decision.
- Hierarchy fits the stated action space: a learned chooser picks "activity + target object", existing engine code executes it (nav GRU, animations). This keeps the learned part at a low decision frequency and sidesteps the sample cost of motor control, but I found no published head-to-head on NPC routines to quantify the benefit.
- The user's headless-lockstep + Linux RTX 3080 setup is in line with published scales for 50k to 2M-parameter nets (8 A100-hours for a Neural MMO 2.0 model; 34 hours for a hide-and-seek stage-4 run). The bottleneck will be simulation throughput, not the GPU.

### Gaps
- No paper found that distills an LLM *decision policy* into a small non-text policy for NPCs/daily life with fidelity metrics (a snippet mentioned a compact recurrent network in a human-AI coordination paper, arXiv 2607.07021, but its abstract page did not confirm that and I did not read the body).
- No published use of DAgger specifically for needs/schedule NPCs.
- Scripted-low-level hierarchical RL in commercial games: no quantitative source found.

## 4. Reward design and pathologies

### Takeaway
Every large system hit a reward pathology: exploiting physics, gaming taxes, following instead of overtaking, dominant strategies under a pure survival reward, hallucination loops. The fixes people used were: symmetric zero-sum terms to kill positive reward cycles, caps and thresholds instead of maximisation, explicit penalty terms on the exploit, curricula and action masks, and populations/scenarios that include adversarial or varied partners. Homeostatic (drive-reduction) rewards have a clean theory and avoid overshoot, but their empirical evidence in multi-agent RL is thin.

### Cited Findings
- Homeostatic RL (Keramati and Gutkin): reward = drive before minus drive after, where drive is a distance from a physiological set point; the policy maximising discounted reward equals the policy minimising discounted drive; reward is an inverted-U in outcome size (overshoot is punished); discounting is necessary (with gamma = 1 only start and end states matter and a long detour scores the same as the shortest path); predicts anticipatory behaviour and risk aversion — [eLife 2014](https://elifesciences.org/articles/04811)
- ForageWorld's reward pays 0.1 per need that stays above half its maximum, deliberately not "maximise each need"; the authors say this correlates with survival time — [ForageWorld](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.06981)
- Neural MMO: survival-only reward (+1 per tick) works as a sanity setting; 2.0 states the broad survival objective "favors dominant strategies"; task predicates return 0-1 floats for dense partial credit (e.g. kill predicate increments of 0.06 per kill, with bonuses on the first and third) — [Neural MMO 1.0](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1903.00784); [Neural MMO 2.0](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.03736)
- GT Sophy: reward is a hand-tuned linear combination (course progress, off-course, wall, loss of traction, passing, collision, unsporting-collision penalties); progress reward alone let the agent follow a fast opponent and bank reward without risk; the passing reward is symmetric (proportional negative reward when an opponent gains ground) so there are "no positive-cycle reward loops to exploit"; training only against the built-in AI made it too aggressive, training against an aggressive opponent made it lose its edge; removing collision-penalty elements made it "much too unsportsmanlike" according to test drivers and stewards; a mixed opponent population was used — [Wurman et al.](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~pstone/Papers/bib2html-links/nature22.pdf)
- Hide-and-seek: agents found physics exploits (box surfing, hiders leaving the play area), count-based exploration degraded with larger state representations, and less environment randomisation produced fewer emergent stages — [Baker et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1909.07528)
- AI Economist: emergent tax-gaming; annealing and entropy regularisation used to stabilise the planner-agent co-adaptation — [Zheng et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2108.02755v1)
- Melting Pot: collective-reward training generally underperformed self-interested training and was less robust to novel partners; tragedy-of-the-commons and free-rider dynamics are by design in Clean Up and Commons Harvest; Coins: no algorithm learned to cooperate — [Melting Pot 2.0](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2211.13746)
- Prosocial behaviour in a food-sharing homeostatic toy model appeared only when agents' internal states were directly coupled to partners' states (affective empathy); observing partner state was not enough (preprint, toy environment) — [Empathic Coupling of Homeostatic States](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.12103v1)
- LLM-agent society failure modes: hallucination cascades that make an agent drop a need such as food, repetitive loops, error propagation through miscommunication — [Project Sid](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2411.00114)
- Needs-based AI in conventional games is score/utility based; a developer post notes purely rational scoring makes routines predictable, so randomness is added (anecdotal, forum) — [Needs-Based AI repo](https://github.com/MaaikeHeyerick/Needs-Based-AI)

### Inferences
- A homeostatic reward for NPC needs should use a bounded, concave per-need term around a set point (penalise both deficit and overshoot) and a discount below 1; ForageWorld's threshold reward is the simplest tested variant. This should avoid the "maximise sleeping/eating" degenerate optimum.
- Starvation of a minority, collusion and oscillation (flipping between two activities every decision) are plausible failure modes but I found no source measuring them in learned needs policies; countermeasures that were reported elsewhere and transfer: add a switching cost or commitment (hysteresis), symmetric terms for any zero-sum interaction, anchoring to a teacher policy, and evaluation with varied partner populations (Melting Pot style) rather than self-play only.
- Because code owns the world truth in the user's design (graph of relationships and property), exploits like duplicating resources are harder than in physics engines, but "gaming the observation" (acting on information the NPC should not have) is the analogue of physics exploits and needs masking.

### Gaps
- No source found quantifying oscillation/dithering between activities in learned needs policies, or starvation inequality between agents in a shared-weight population.
- Reward hacking specific to multi-agent *homeostatic* rewards beyond the Chalmers thesis and the empathic-coupling toy paper: not found.

## 5. Inference cost: per-agent, per-decision, batching, decision frequency, runtimes

### Takeaway
Published policies in this class are tiny (50k to 2M parameters) and the papers say little about CPU latency; the only primary latency figure I found is GT Sophy's 23-30 ms end-to-end for a 4x2048 network including asynchronous communication. Decision frequency in successful systems is low (10 Hz for racing; about 7.5 Hz for OpenAI Five; seconds for LLM social goals), which is why per-agent cost stays small. Hard CPU benchmarks for small MLP/GRU/attention nets in game runtimes were not found; estimates below are arithmetic.

### Cited Findings
- GT Sophy acts at 10 Hz (every 100 ms); the authors tested 5-60 Hz and "found no substantial performance gains from acting more frequently than 10 Hz"; the agent ran on a separate computer and talked to the game over HTTP/wired Ethernet because Gran Turismo Sport runs only on PS4; "asynchronous communication and inference takes around 23-30 ms, depending on the size of the network" — [Wurman et al.](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~pstone/Papers/bib2html-links/nature22.pdf)
- Network sizes for reference: Neural MMO 1.0 50-100k parameters; hide-and-seek about 1.6M; GT Sophy 4 x 2,048 ReLU layers; OpenAI Five about 159M (84% in one 4,096-unit LSTM); ForageWorld 512-unit GRU pruned to 90% sparsity — see sources in sections 1-2
- OpenAI Five: 30 fps game, one action every 4th frame (about 0.133 s, 7.5 steps/s); forward-pass GPUs run batches of about 60; per-step inference cost not stated — [OpenAI Five](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1912.06680)
- Neural MMO 2.0 engine: about 3,000 agent steps per CPU core per second for the simulator alone (random actions), versus 800-1,000 in 1.x; the paper estimates about 250M agent steps (about 2.5 TB at roughly 10 KB per observation) per core-day — [Neural MMO 2.0](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.03736)
- Project Sid regenerates social goals every 5-10 s; reflex modules are small non-LLM nets; no per-agent LLM cost reported — [Project Sid](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2411.00114)
- RTNeural (C++ inference for real-time audio): supports Dense, GRU, LSTM, Conv1D; Eigen, xsimd and STL backends; compile-time architecture gives better speed; outperforms libtorch for small layers while libtorch scales better for larger ones; tested on an i7-9750H; the paper reports a real-time factor on audio at 48 kHz in figures only, no per-layer microsecond numbers in the text — [RTNeural](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.03037)
- MAgent: up to one million agents on a single GPU server (abstract) — [MAgent](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.00600)
- Mean-field MARL: the Q-function size is independent of agent count — [Yang et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1802.05438)
- Small LMs as NPC brains (for contrast): inZOI's "Smart Zoi" uses an on-device 0.5B-parameter Mistral NeMo Minitron small language model via NVIDIA ACE, requires a GeForce RTX GPU, early access since 2025-03-28; GamesRadar questioned how much of the visible behaviour is actually driven by the model — [NVIDIA announcement](https://www.nvidia.com/en-sg/geforce/news/nvidia-ace-naraka-bladepoint-inzoi-launch-this-month/); [GamesRadar](https://www.gamesradar.com/games/simulation/the-sims-rival-inzoi-uses-special-nvidia-ai-tech-to-power-its-characters-but-this-kinda-just-looks-like-regular-npc-ai-to-me)

### Inferences (arithmetic, not measurements)
- FLOPs per forward pass are about 2 x parameters for dense layers. A 100k-parameter policy is about 0.2 MFLOP per decision; a 1M-parameter one about 2 MFLOP; GT Sophy's policy trunk (3 hidden-to-hidden layers of 2,048 x 2,048) is about 12.6M weights, about 25 MFLOP. At a pessimistic 5-20 GFLOP/s of effective single-core throughput for unbatched small matrices, 0.2 MFLOP is about 10-40 microseconds and 2 MFLOP is about 0.1-0.4 ms; batching 1,000 agents into one matrix multiply raises efficiency, so 1,000 x 0.2 MFLOP = 200 MFLOP is on the order of a few milliseconds on a multi-core CPU. These are consistent with the user's existing nets ("fractions of a millisecond per step").
- With event-driven decisions (when an activity ends, a need crosses a threshold, or an event happens) rather than per tick, 1,000 NPCs deciding once per 30-120 game seconds is roughly 10-35 decisions per second at 1x speed, which is negligible for a sub-millisecond net, whereas the Strands Decider at 0.03-0.1 s per decision (the user's figure) would need roughly 0.3-3.5 GPU-seconds per real second for the same load, before batching.
- Decision frequency evidence (GT Sophy found no gain above 10 Hz for a fast-dynamics task) suggests that for slow social/needs decisions even much lower rates are enough; the real cost driver becomes training-time simulation throughput, not inference.
- Attention over an entity set scales as O(n^2) in set size: keep candidate sets capped (for example top-K nearest or relevant objects) so the per-agent cost stays near the MLP figures above.

### Gaps
- No primary source with measured microseconds per inference for 100k-1M parameter MLP/GRU/attention nets on CPU in a game engine (ONNX Runtime, ggml, Unity Sentis, custom). RTNeural gives only real-time-factor plots.
- Third-party pages claim PufferLib reaches 1-4M steps/s, but these are unverified secondary summaries and I did not rely on them.
- No numbers on batching strategy for hundreds of NPCs in a shipped game.

## 6. Games and industry

### Takeaway
Shipped learned-policy NPCs exist for racing (Forza Drivatar since 2005; GT Sophy opponents in Gran Turismo 7) but I found no shipped game using a learned (RL or imitation) policy for crowd or NPC daily life. Recent life-sim attempts (inZOI Smart Zoi) use a small on-device language model rather than a non-text policy; Ubisoft La Forge's RL work is documented for bots (Hyper Scape testing) and internships, not for shipped crowds. Conventional needs systems remain utility or score based.

### Cited Findings
- GT Sophy: developed by Sony AI, Polyphony Digital and SIE; beat top drivers in 2021 events on Gran Turismo Sport; training details in section 3; a GamesBeat piece says Sophy debuted for Gran Turismo 7 and (secondary, conflicting with the Nature paper) that training ran about two weeks on 20 PS5 consoles — the Nature paper says 10-20 PS4 consoles — [Sony GT Sophy page](https://www.gran-turismo.com/us/gran-turismo-sophy/); [GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/the-breakthrough-ai-racing-agent-gt-sophy-debuts-for-gran-turismo-7/); [Nature paper PDF](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~pstone/Papers/bib2html-links/nature22.pdf)
- Forza Drivatar: conceived at Microsoft Research Cambridge; early titles (from Forza Motorsport 1, 2005) used a Bayesian neural network learning from the player's driving; since Forza 5 it is described as reinforcement-learning-based with cloud data; Turn 10's Dan Greenawalt says it now bears "very little resemblance to the original" and mixes learning with hand-written logic (secondary sources; Microsoft Research pages no longer live) — [Dexerto summary of Ars Technica](https://www.dexerto.com/racing/forza-developers-reveal-how-they-make-super-realistic-ai-drivers-1419779/); [JIKGUARD](https://www.jikguard.com/news/how-forzas-drivatar-actually-works)
- Ubisoft La Forge: a data scientist said RL was tested in Hyper Scape to build bots that play more like humans, with reward and penalty shaping; current internship postings target LLM/VLM plus RL or evolutionary strategies for NPC decision-making under data scarcity (intent, not shipped results) — [80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/ubisoft-explained-how-it-makes-npcs-more-realistic/); [Ubisoft internship listing](https://hitmarker.net/jobs/ubisoft-research-internship-improving-non-player-character-decision-making-with-ml-f-m-nb-1578259)
- inZOI (Krafton): Smart Zoi uses a 0.5B on-device small LM (NVIDIA ACE); players give life goals; a Krafton-linked interview says it can explain its choices — [NVIDIA](https://www.nvidia.com/en-sg/geforce/news/nvidia-ace-naraka-bladepoint-inzoi-launch-this-month/); [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/games/life-sim/inzoi-is-creating-a-newfangled-life-sim-npc-that-can-grow-and-develop-its-own-personality-with-nvidias-ai-tech)
- Hobby/open source: Catagochi-ML is an RL agent with hunger, energy, affection inputs and a priority layer on top of the learned policy (hybrid) — [itch.io](https://paugonzalez.itch.io/catagochi-ml); search for RL needs-driven NPC routines otherwise returned rule/utility systems ("traditional AI remains the backbone ... reliability, interpretability, low computational cost") — [AI in gaming overview](https://aiwiki.ai/wiki/ai_in_gaming/raw)
- Agent-based modelling with neural parts: AgentTorch (MIT, 8.4M agents, differentiable, archetype-based LLM behaviour) and FLAME GPU 2 (up to 16M-agent Sugarscape-style model) — see section 1 sources
- Research-scale platforms: Neural MMO (128-agent maps, 3,000 agent steps per core-second), MAgent (up to 1M agents per GPU server), Melting Pot (50+ substrates), JaxMARL (GPU-vectorised environments) — see sections 1 and 3

### Inferences
- The two best-documented shipping precedents (Drivatar, GT Sophy) are narrow-domain, continuous-control agents trained offline with heavy infrastructure and shipped with a fixed network; the user's plan (small nets trained offline, weights as a file) matches that deployment model. Neither covers needs, goals or relationships.
- A learned non-text society policy would be novel in shipped games as far as I could find; the nearest commercial effort (inZOI) chose an LM, at 0.5B parameters about three orders of magnitude larger than the policies discussed here and requiring an RTX-class GPU.

### Gaps
- Not researched or not found: EA SEED, Unity ML-Agents case studies, Modl.ai, Microsoft/Ninja Theory WHAM (no NPC-population material located), any 2024-2026 shipped title with learned crowd or daily-life policies, GDC talks on learned utility weights. Absence of results is not proof of absence.
- La Forge's Hyper Scape result is from a single trade-press interview and the extent of deployment is unclear.
